# 10.10 The digits of Java's `DecimalFormat` for a double filter literal

- **Status:** in review
- **Needs:** 10.7 (its finding C2)
- **Owns:** `crates/oxhaven-engine/src/ops/filter/` (the new `decimal_format.rs`,
  `condition.rs`, `mod.rs`, the fixtures `java_decimal_format.txt` and `java_formats.txt`)
- **Read:** nothing from the JDK's source. The owner ruled on 2026-11-18 that no code derived
  from OpenJDK source enters the repository; this task works from documentation, published
  papers and what Java prints (see Sources)

## Scope

A structured filter comparison of a column with a double prints the literal as Deephaven does,
with Java's `new DecimalFormat("##0.################").format(double)`
(`FilterFactory#generateNumericConditionFilter`). Before this task doubles from 2^53 and exact
ties at the sixteenth fraction digit were refused with
`unported!("java.text.DecimalFormat#format")`, and about 1 in 400 random doubles of many digits
got a last digit that differs from Java's (10.7, item C2).

Find, as a black box, the digits Java's formatter starts from and how it rounds them, and
format every double that way.

## Notes

### The rules found

Java's formatter takes decimal digits from a binary to decimal conversion (the one
`Double.toString` used before JDK 19) and rounds them to sixteen fraction digits. For a finite
positive double `m * 2^e`, with `m` of 53 bits (fewer for a subnormal):

1. **A whole double from 2^52 and below 2^63** (`e` from 0 to 10) keeps the digits of the
   number itself, less its last digit where doubles are 64, 128 or 256 apart and its last two
   where they are 512 or 1024 apart, rounded half up. `2^60` is `1152921504606846976` and
   prints as `1152921504606846980`.
2. **Any other double** gets free-format digits in the manner of Steele and White: digits of
   the exact value are written until what remains is less than half the gap to the next double
   (`low`), or the digits written and rounded up are less than half a gap above the value
   (`high`); with only `high` the last digit is rounded up, with both it goes to the nearer
   decimal. Java's differences from the shortest digits that read back:
   - both comparisons are strict, so a decimal exactly half a gap away is not taken: `1e23` is
     `99999999999999991611392`, `10^23` is exactly half a gap above it, and Java prints
     `99999999999999990000000`;
   - when both hold and the rest is exactly a half, the last digit goes to the even digit
     (`2174429028029277.25` gives `...277.2`, `576558572665954.75` gives `...954.8`);
   - the first digit is written without either test, which changes the result only for
     subnormals of a few bits (the least double has the digits `49`, not `5`); a value within
     half a gap of the next power of ten starts from a zero digit, so that `2 * 2^-1074`
     (9.88e-324) has the digit `1`;
   - a mantissa of a single bit counts half the gap on both sides. For a normal power of two
     the double below is that close; Java does the same for subnormals of one bit, where it is
     not (`32 * 2^-1074` has the digits `158`, not `16`);
   - in the binade from 2^84 (`e` = 32) the `high` test fails when a sum Java holds in a
     signed 64-bit integer passes `i64::MAX`, and the digits are then not rounded up:
     `26805587305293154973908992` prints as `26805587305293154000000000`. The model (the rest
     and the half gap in units of `10 / 5^25` of a digit, wrapping at the seventeenth digit)
     explains all 1,643 such doubles among 60,000 random ones of the binade. No other binade
     shows it.
3. **Rounding to sixteen fraction digits** looks at the first digit dropped: below five down,
   above five or a five with digits after it up. A five that ends the digits is a tie only when
   the digits are the double's exact value, and goes to the even digit; otherwise the side of
   the digits on which the value lies decides (`7.4999999999999996e-16` has the digits `75`
   and gives `0.0000000000000007`; `8.5000000000000001e-16` has `85` and gives
   `0.0000000000000009`).

### Hypotheses tried, and what the samples showed

The digits were separated from the rounding by also printing each double with a
`DecimalFormat` of 340 fraction digits, which rounds nothing.

| # | Hypothesis | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H0 | the digits are the shortest that read back, nearest the value | wrong for 308 of 100,000 doubles uniform over bit patterns, all from 2^53 to 2^71 but two in the binade of 2^84; for 28% of doubles from 2^50 to 2^71; for about 345 of the 2,046 normal powers of two; for the least subnormals |
| H1 | H0, and whole doubles below 2^63 keep their own digits less 0, 1 or 2 by gap, half up | right for every whole double below 2^63; the others above remain |
| H2a | free-format digits with strict tests, last digit half way rounded up | wrong for 342 of 100,000 doubles with binary exponents from -80 to 80 (those ending `.25`, `.75` and the like at the seventeenth digit) |
| H2b | the same, half way rounded down | wrong for 304 of 100,000 (those where the digit before is odd) |
| H2c | the same, half way to the even digit; powers of two counting the gap below on both sides | right except subnormals of a few bits (8 of the least 399) and the binade of 2^84 |
| H3 | H2c, the first digit untested, a zero digit before a value within half a gap of a power of ten, half the gap for subnormals of one bit | right for every subnormal up to 30,000 times the least and 39,000 others |
| A1 | in the binade of 2^84 the digit is kept whenever the rest of the seventeenth digit is above a bound | predicted 2,914 of 60,000 where Java has 1,643: it ignored that many stop at the sixteenth digit |
| A2 | there the sum of rest and half gap wraps in 64 bits (rule 2, last item) | right for 60,000 of 60,000 |
| R0 | the digits are rounded half even | wrong for 9,824 of 19,365 doubles whose digits end on the five |
| R1 | a five that ends the digits follows the side of the value (rule 3) | right for all of them |

### Samples

All from JDK 21 (`openjdk-21`, the only JDK on the machine) through a small Java program that
prints the two formats for bit patterns read from standard input, compared with this module in
a scratch binary built with optimisation outside the repository.

- While forming the rules: 1,384,000 doubles in 13 families; no mismatch with the final code.
- Fresh after the rules were fixed, no mismatch in any: 1,000,000 uniform over bit patterns;
  5,700,000 at 10,000 and 15,000 per binade from 2^-127 to 2^100 (a seventh of them with
  trailing zero bits); every mantissa of at most 12 bits in the binades from 2^-140 to 2^110
  (514,048); 2,000,000 with mantissas of 13 to 40 bits in the same binades; 500,000 with
  binary exponents from -80 to 80; 300,000 in each of: whole numbers and halves up to 2^66,
  doubles from 2^50 to 2^71, the binade of 2^84, mantissas of at most four bits at any
  exponent; 298,985 decimals of at most 17 digits and their neighbours; 450,000 around the sixteenth
  fraction digit; 250,000 ties and near ties there; 409,053 subnormals; 200,000 negative
  doubles. 12,522,086 lines in all.
- Again with the code as committed, no mismatch: 1,000,000 uniform over bit patterns, 456,000
  at 2,000 per binade from 2^-127 to 2^100, 100,000 in the binade of 2^84, 125,000 ties and
  near ties. 1,681,000 lines.
- The fixture given with the task (`java_formats.txt`, 1,981 doubles) and the one of 10.7
  (`java_doubles.txt`, 443, which this task no longer skips any line of): no mismatch.

`java_decimal_format.txt` is the committed fixture: 3,558 doubles with both formats as Java
printed them, chosen by rule (each exponent of rule 1; powers of two where the shortest digits
differ and where they do not; every subnormal of one bit and the least 300; decimals half a gap
away; last digits half way; the binade of 2^84 with and without the wrap and its neighbours;
each case of rule 3; random and negative doubles).

### What stays refused, and what is not known

- Nothing is refused: the `unported!("java.text.DecimalFormat#format")` call is gone, and the
  filter files call `unported!` for nothing in this path.
- The binade of 2^84 is the only place where Java's 64-bit arithmetic was seen to show. The
  samples bound any other such place: none at 25,000 doubles per binade between 2^-127 and
  2^100, and outside those binades (about 700 random doubles each) the numbers involved do not
  fit 64 bits.
- Only JDK 21 was observed. Deephaven runs on JDK 11 to 25; the documentation gives no reason
  for the format to differ between them, but it was not checked.

### Sources

- Observation of JDK 21's output, as described above. Running the JDK only: its source was not
  opened, fetched or searched for, and neither was branch `task/10.7-unported-filter-literals`
  or its `dtoa.rs`.
- The public documentation of `java.text.DecimalFormat` (rounding mode half even by default)
  and of `Double.toString` before JDK 19 ("at least one digit to represent the fractional
  part, and beyond that as many, but only as many, more digits as are needed to uniquely
  distinguish the argument value from adjacent values of type double"), and the fact, stated
  with JDK 19's change of `Double.toString`, that the earlier conversion does not always give
  the shortest decimal. These were used from memory, not fetched.
- G. L. Steele and J. L. White, "How to print floating-point numbers accurately" (1990): the
  free-format loop with its `low` and `high` tests, from which rule 2 departs as listed.
- `tasks/10.7-unported-filter-literals.md` on main, item C2.

The author is a language model and cannot exclude having seen JDK source in training. No rule
here was written from a recollection of it: each is in the table above with the sample that
showed it, and the two that look least like a design (the table of rule 1, the wrap of rule 2)
are stated as observations with their evidence.
