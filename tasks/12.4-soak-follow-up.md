## Scope

Task 12.3 left two things open in the soak test, and this task settles them.

1. Processor time per round rose by 7 to 8% over the fitted 30 minutes in both 45-minute
   runs (the limit is 20%). Is it a trend or does it settle? If a trend, find the cause.
2. Resident memory takes 9 to 13 minutes to level, in steps of 10 to 20 MB. What are the
   steps?

Fix what they turn out to be if it is small and local. No workflow changes, no new
dependencies, no limit loosened.

## Notes

### Answers

1. **The processor time is not a trend of the server. It follows the machine.** In two
   hours the cost of a round moved between 0.29 and 0.32 s in plateaus, not along a line,
   and a server started fresh beside the one that was 105 minutes old cost the same as the
   old one. Nothing in the server was found that grows or gets slower with the number of
   cycles or rows.
2. **The steps of resident memory are transparent huge pages.** The allocator asks the
   kernel for them in the gigabyte it reserves, and the kernel gives and takes them in
   pieces of 2 MB on its own schedule. They are not memory the server holds. The soak now
   runs its server without them, and the resident size is then flat to within 3 MB.

Found on the way and fixed: **a session kept every export id it had released**, one entry
each, for as long as it lived (below). And two checks of the soak itself were wrong and
failed on a correct server in a run longer than 45 minutes.

- It falls for 40 minutes, jumps between minutes 45 and 50, and then stays between 0.31
  and 0.32 s for 70 minutes. A structure that degrades with the number of cycles would
  give a slope, and the last 70 minutes have none.
- Between minutes 45 and 50 there were 24 rounds above 0.5 s, the system time of the
  update graph's thread doubled, and the longest time between two cycles was 0.76 s. From
  the same minute on, a fixed loop **in another process** needed a third more processor
  time (24 to 33 ms) and never came back. The server's own cost rose by less (8%).
  In those 70 minutes the guest's page cache also fell from 12.4 to 2.5 GB without anything
  in the guest asking for it. The machine changed, not the server. The guest has no
  hardware counters (`perf_event_open` fails), so what changed in it cannot be said.
- **A fresh server beside the old one costs the same.** Started at minute 105 with the same
  workload and seed, for 329 rounds: fresh 0.318 s per round, the old one over the same
  rounds 0.317 s (medians 0.32 and 0.32). The old one alone, in the ten minutes before:
  0.307. So two servers at once cost each 3% more, and 105 minutes of age cost nothing.
- Over the 16 windows of 30 minutes of the run (one every 5 minutes), the statistic the
  soak judges was between -7.0% and +14.8%. The 7 to 8% of task 12.3 are inside what the

Because the cause was not in the server, there was nothing to narrow down with `--skip`.
What was read instead, looking for anything kept per cycle or per row: the writable and
tracked column sources, the time, ring and input tables, the update graph's subscriber
lists and stepping, the server's update graph driver, the session store, and the Barrage
exchange and producer. All of them prune. The released ids of a session did not; that is
the fix below, and its cost is memory, not processor time.

**In the soak now:** each round also times a fixed piece of work in the driver
(`control_s`), and the summary prints it beside the processor time, so that a rise of the
server's processor time can be held against the machine's. It is reported and not judged:
the two do not move in proportion.

### Resident memory

With huge pages (the two-hour run): start 150.4 MB, after the warm-up 148.8, end 186.8,
peak 200.3; fitted +12.7 MB per hour over the whole run, and over the windows of 30
minutes between -10.7 and +38.4, **3 of 16 above the limit of 24**. So the long soak as
task 12.3 left it would have failed about one night in five on a correct server.

What moves:

- All growth of anonymous memory is in one mapping: the 1 GiB arena that mimalloc
  reserves. Everything else of the process stays at 55.8 to 55.9 MB for two hours.
- mimalloc marks the arena with `madvise(MADV_HUGEPAGE)` (its option `allow_thp`, on by
  default), and the machine's setting for transparent huge pages is `madvise`, so the
  kernel may back the arena with pages of 2 MB. It does so in two ways. On a page fault,
  when a churn table of 50,000 rows touches a fresh region, a whole 2 MB becomes resident
  at once; a second later mimalloc returns the freed part (`purge_delay`), which splits
  the huge page again. That is the movement of 8 to 18 MB from minute to minute. And
  `khugepaged` goes through the arena about every eleven minutes (4,096 pages every 10 s)
  and collapses regions that have any page resident into huge pages (it may fill 511
  absent pages of 512): 30 MB more in 30 seconds at minute 22 of the run, for example.
  Those are the steps, and their period is why levelling takes 9 to 13 minutes.
- The resident size follows `AnonHugePages` of the process (correlation 0.86 over the two
  hours). The part in huge pages went from a median of 42 MB to 82 MB; the part in small
  pages stayed between 33 and 51 MB.

Without huge pages (`MIMALLOC_ALLOW_THP=0`, which makes mimalloc turn them off for the
process and changes nothing else), one hour on the base server, with builds and tests
beside it: start 81.8 MB, after the warm-up 110.9, end 113.2, peak 121.8; fitted +2.6 MB
per hour, and between +0.7 and +3.2 over the windows of 30 minutes. After minute 15 the
samples have a standard deviation of 3.1 MB (with huge pages: 9.3). The resident size is
at 110 MB after about ten minutes: the long-lived tables fill, and the allocator's pools
reach their size.

So this is not a defect of the server, and nothing in the server was changed for it. It is
a property of the allocator on a machine that allows huge pages on request: about 50 to
60 MB more resident, and moving ("The final tree" below, on processor time).

**In the soak now:** the server it starts runs without transparent huge pages unless
`--huge-pages keep` is given, and each round records `huge_kb`. The memory limit then
judges what the server holds. `docs/SOAK.md`, "Memory", has the reasoning and what the
limits are now.

### The fix: released export ids

`Session` kept the ids of released exports in a `BTreeSet<i32>`, so that a released id is
refused when it is used again. Nothing was ever removed, so a session grew by one entry per
export it had ever released. Java keeps the same ids as a row set, which is ranges
(`SessionState.releasedExports`), and a client releases its ids roughly in the order it
made them. Now ranges here as well: a map from the first id of a run to its last, joined on
insert.

- Test: `released_ids_are_remembered_as_ranges_however_many_there_were`
  (`crates/oxhaven-server/src/session/tests.rs`): 3,000 client exports released in reverse
  within batches, and 300 server-side ones, must leave two ranges, and every id must still
  be refused. Before the fix (with the count read from the set) it fails at the count.
- Measured on release servers without huge pages, one session that makes and releases
  2.1 million exports: base 42.2 to 69.6 MB, in a line, 13 to 15 bytes per id; with the fix
  45.5 to 48.9 MB, flat after the first 900,000. A released id used again is refused as
  before (`FAILED_PRECONDITION`, "Export in state RELEASED").
- In the soak's workload the long-lived sessions release about 90 exports a round,
  which is about 2 MB per hour. **The soak does not show the fix**: the final run, on the
  fixed server, fitted +1.9 MB per hour, and the hour on the base server +2.6; the
  medians of five minutes wander by 1.5 MB around 112 MB in both, so a slope of that size
  over 30 minutes is noise. The measurement above is the evidence.

### Two checks of the soak that were wrong

Both failed on a correct server, only after a long time, and both are how Java behaves.

- **The sum over the tail of the time table** (`time_tail_agg`). The values summed were
  milliseconds with a fraction. An aggregation keeps its sum by adding and subtracting
  (`DoubleChunkedSumOperator` does the same), and after 32 minutes that sum differed in
  the last bit from one made of the rows as they are. The soak's own rule is that sums are
  over whole numbers; this one was not. Now `(double) epochMillis(Timestamp)`. Starting the
  time table at a whole millisecond was not enough: `epochNanos(Timestamp) / 1000000` is a
  division of doubles, and nanoseconds since 1970 need 61 bits.
- **The two counts over the two time tables** (`blink_behind`). They were compared with
  each other, allowing the blink table to be behind. But each time table reads the clock in
  its own refresh (`TimeTable#refresh`), so one can be a row ahead of the other for a
  cycle, in either direction: twice in two hours here. Each count is now checked exactly
  against its own table: rows made are (last minus first) / period + 1.

The checks are 24 kinds now (were 23). Both changes replace a comparison that tolerated or
missed something by one that is exact.

### The Barrage copy that lost a row (pull request 182)

The short soak of the pull request failed once: the copy the subscriber builds of
`agg_by_sym` had the row of `S03` with 60 trades where the table had 54, for the whole 15
seconds, and was right again a round later. Everything else in the run was within limits.

- **Not a race of the check, and not the server.** The world of seed 1 is the same in
  every run, so the row of `S03` after each change can be computed from the seed alone:
  the copy had the row as of the add of the ninth write of round 33, and the table as of
  the delete of the tenth. What differs between runs is only where the server cuts its
  messages (one a second at most). A pause of 1.3 s before the delete of the ninth write
  puts that delete, the tenth add (which does not touch `S03`) and the tenth delete into
  one message, and **the violation then appears every time**, with the same numbers as in
  CI.
- **The message is right.** For that update the client reports 20 modified rows for
  `SumQty`, `N` and `AvgQty`, `S03` among them, and 19 for `MinPx`, `MaxPx` and `MaxTs`.
- **The client's union of them is wrong**: `all_modified_rows` has 19 rows, without
  `S03`, and `TableUpdate.modified()` reads exactly those rows. The union is made by
  `RowSequenceBuilder::AddInterval` (deephaven-core, `cpp-client/deephaven/dhcore/src/container/row_sequence.cc`):
  when the new interval starts inside one it has, it takes that one's start and drops
  the rest of it, without keeping its end. Adding [0,7) to [0,20) leaves [0,7). The
  columns are added in order, so the union is right again after `AvgQty` and wrong again
  after `MaxTs`.
- **The check was wrong since task 12.3**, on `main` as well; nothing in this branch
  touches it. It needs a message of several cycles in which a row is modified only in
  columns before the last, which is rare with 20 symbols that nearly every add touches.
- **Fixed in the check, by making it take what the update says**: the copy applies the
  modified cells column by column (`modified_rows`, `after_modifies`), and reports a
  modified row it never had. The forced run passes, and so do three rounds after it.
  Nothing is retried, loosened or skipped.
- Evidence: `bench/results/2026-11-18-task-12.4-soak-replica-union.txt` (the violation,
  the row after each change, the forced run before and after, the client's row counts).
  After the fix the short profile passed 12 times of 12 on the debug server, three at a
  time on the four cores.

### Handover

- **A defect of the Deephaven client to report upstream**: `RowSequenceBuilder::AddInterval`
  loses the tail of an interval that contains the start of the one added, so
  `TickingUpdate::AllModifiedRows` and with it `TableUpdate.modified()` and
  `modified_prev()` of `pydeephaven_ticking` 42.9 can leave out rows. It is not ours to
  fix, and a Java server triggers it as well. Anything of ours that reads a ticking
  subscription through `modified()` has the same hole; the soak was the only such reader
  in `bench/` and `oracle/`.

- **Decisions for the orchestrator.** (a) The soak's server runs without huge pages by
  default. It is one commit (`bench: run the soak's server without transparent huge
  pages`) and can be dropped; then the long limit of 24 MB per hour fails about one run in
  five. (b) The two changed checks compare something else than before; by PORTING rule 3
  that is the orchestrator's to accept.
- **For a deployment** (not done here, it changes what an operator sees): mimalloc's
  huge pages cost 50 to 60 MB of resident memory on this workload and make the resident
  size move by tens of MB. `MIMALLOC_ALLOW_THP=0` in the server's environment turns them
  off. Whether the server should do that itself is a question for task 9.13's owner; the
  benchmark of 9.13 would have to say what it costs.
- **A longer nightly profile is not warranted** by the processor time: two hours showed no
  ageing, and the fresh-beside-old comparison is the stronger test and takes ten minutes.
  What two hours did find were the two wrong checks; with them fixed, 45 minutes cover the
  same ground. If the machines allow it, running the long profile once a week for two hours
  (`--duration 7200`) costs nothing but time.
- **The long memory limit could be halved** now that the server runs without huge pages
  (seven windows of 30 minutes between +0.7 and +4.2 MB per hour). It was left at 24:
  the evidence is from this one machine. A few nightly runs will say.
- **Not done:** the count of live exports and sessions is still not exposed (as in 12.3).
- The branch is behind `origin/main` by the commits of #181 and one runlog entry; they
  touch no file this task touches, and the branch was not merged with them.
